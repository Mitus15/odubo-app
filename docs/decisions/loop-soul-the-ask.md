## How it works

**Fifteen messages a day, sent one at a time, each with a name in it.**

Not a broadcast. Not a group chat. Not a story. A person opening a message
that was clearly written to them says yes at a rate nothing else comes close
to; the same words sent to forty people at once converts near zero. It is the
same fifteen minutes either way.

Build the list first — open your contacts, Instagram followers, the Scott's
staff schedule, church, TRU, the band's circles — and just write names down.
Aim for 100. Do not filter. "They probably wouldn't come" is not your call to
make on someone's behalf, and being asked is a kindness even when the answer
is no.

**Track it crudely.** A note with three columns: *asked · yes · maybe*. That
note is the marketing dashboard. Nothing else is.

## The messages

Short is the point. Three or four lines. A long message reads as a flyer; a
short one reads as a friend. Always end on a **question they can answer in one
word** — "can you make it?" beats "let me know."

> Hey [name] — I'm playing my album for the first time on Saturday the 26th at
> Scott's. Nobody's heard it yet. Doors 6:30, album at 8, $5 at the door.

> I'd really love you there. Can you make it?

The headcount line is true and it gives them permission to decline, which is
exactly why it gets answers.

## Two rules

**Never send the same person the same message twice.** If they didn't answer,
the follow-up is G, not a resend.

**Do not defend the event.** If someone can't come, "no worries, next one" and
move on. Persuading costs more time than the next ten names, and the next ten
names are where the yeses are.
